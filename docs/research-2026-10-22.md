## 1. Your questions, answered

### Is the layered decision path optimal? Couldn't d1 go first?
The order is right. Clean-up, fixed phrases and pattern rules together take under 0.05 ms, so trying them first
costs nothing even when none fits, and saves the ~0.2–0.65 s d1 call whenever one does. Putting d1 first was
tested on the 107 original sentences: 90/107 right (vs 107/107), and slower on every command. d1 also can't
produce titles or numbers (it only scores the options it's given), so rules would still run after it.

### "Previous episode became next episode" — model or configuration?
Configuration. d1 never sees an option's name, only its description ("A <description>", "B …"). There was no
option for previous episode, start over, jump to a time or set the volume to a number, and "skip" was listed
under the playback option, so d1 picked the nearest match. On top of that, the code turned the episode intent
into "+1" regardless of what was said. With one described option per action (22), d1 on its own went from 84 to
100 of the 107 sentences, without changing the model (details in §2.2).

### What does each stage add? (end of speech → action)
| Stage | Time | When |
|---|---|---|
| TV app waits for silence | 700 ms | every command (the biggest fixed cost) |
| Upload of the clip | a few ms (~100 KB on the LAN) *(estimate)* | every command |
| Speech-to-text (Parakeet TDT v2, CPU) | 85 ms for 1.5 s of speech, 122 ms for 2.5 s, 186 ms for 4 s, 225 ms for 5 s | every command |
| Clean-up / fixed phrases / patterns | 0.002 ms / <0.001 ms / 0.006–0.04 ms | every command |
| d1 | ~0.65 s | only when no rule fits (~4% of commands now) |
| The action (your activity log) | 70–180 ms pause/volume/apps; ~1.4 s YouTube search; 4.5–8.4 s to start a film or episode | |

### Hold to talk instead of waiting for silence?
- The remote's mic button sends press and release 0.3 ms apart whether you tap or hold (Phase 0), so the TV
  can't see how long it's held. That is the Android TV Voice "on-request" mode, where the TV, not the button,
  ends the audio. Hold-to-talk mode (the remote sends "stop" on release) exists in the protocol but only when the
  TV advertises it; nothing published says TCL's does *(unverified)*.
- Already available: press the mic button **again** while talking to send at once (skips the 700 ms).
- Worth a test: hold, say "one two three", let go, keep saying "four five six". If the transcript stops at
  "three", the remote stops sending on release; Android's remote-audio layer then delivers exact zeros, which the
  app can detect and treat as "done" *(inference from AOSP source)*.
- The bigger win either way is streaming + an earlier, smarter end of speech (§2.3).

### Streaming the audio instead of sending it at the end?
Yes. Its value is mostly that the laptop can decide **while you're still in the 700 ms pause**: decode at ~150 ms
of silence, act at once if the words already form a complete command ("volume twenty", "… episode 3"), wait
~0.5 s if they could continue ("play Pluribus season 1"), fall back to ~0.7 s if nothing parses. Estimated end of
speech → action: ~0.25–0.4 s instead of ~0.85–1.05 s. Design in §2.3.

### Kodi, Just Player, Stremio's own player, or something else?
Decided and done: **Kodi as the default, with the agent still choosing the stream.** From the laptop, Kodi's JSON-RPC is
the only player API that covers start position, subtitles from your add-ons by link (with names and languages),
picking audio/subtitle tracks per title, exact seeking and progress. Just Player can't receive subtitle links
over ADB on Android 14 (`am start` can't pass Uri lists). Stremio's own player is the deepest integration but took
15–20 s to start cold, can't take per-title language rules, and loads add-on subtitles through its local server,
which has an open boot bug. Letting Stremio hand off to an external player drops your subtitle add-ons (Stremio
doesn't pass subtitles to external players). NuvioTV and similar apps use Stremio add-ons but don't sync your
Stremio library. Browsing stays in Stremio; "open X in Stremio" opens its page. Details in §2.1.

### The best stream: quality, bitrate, ratings, cached?
Done. AIOStreams sends structured details (`streamData`) to an AIOStreams-compatible client, which the agent now
identifies as: parsed resolution, quality, codec, HDR/DV, audio, languages, release group, size, duration, cache
state, whether it's already in your Premiumize library, and **its score** (the ★ in its names, from your ranked
expressions: "Web T1", "4K", "DV", "DD+", "Hulu"…). The order is now:
1. the audio the title should play in (English; Spanish for Spanish-made titles; Latino just below Castilian)
2. resolution (≤ `max_height`)
3. AIOStreams' score (★)
4. already in your Premiumize library
5. bitrate (≤ `max_mbps`, 60 Mbit/s for the TV's 100 Mbps port)

Dropped: not cached, cam/TS, above the resolution or bitrate cap. Also fixed: AIOStreams redirects a link that
can't play to a short placeholder video (`/static/downloading.mp4`…), which the old link check accepted. Examples:
*The Bear* S3E2 → 4K Hulu WEB-DL by FLUX, ★3625, 16 Mbit/s, in your library; *La Casa de Papel* S1E1 → Spanish
1080p Blu-ray REMUX, ★4755.

### Can it use Cinemeta, SubSense, OpenSubtitles PRO, AIOStreams, AIOMetadata?
Yes; they all speak the same add-on protocol and the agent reads your installed add-ons from your Stremio
account (so their private config links never need to be copied anywhere):
- **AIOStreams:** streams + structured data (in use); also a JSON search API (§2.4).
- **SubSense, OpenSubtitles PRO:** subtitle links per language, ranked by how well they match the file name.
  In use with Kodi: for *The Bear* S3E2 they returned 17 subtitles (English, Spanish) in 1.1 s.
- **AIOMetadata:** TMDB-based search finds Spanish and alternative titles ("juego de tronos") that Cinemeta can
  miss; proposed as the first title search with Cinemeta as fallback.
- **Cinemeta:** stays (episode lists, fallback search).

### Would a cloud speech-to-text (Gemini…) help?
Not for speed: the fastest services finalize in ~85–130 ms after speech ends (measured from the US, connection
already open); add 30–60 ms from Spain and they tie with local Parakeet. Gemini's batch transcription takes
1.7–2.3 s. Some are more accurate on accents and names (keyterm lists of your library titles), at under $2/month
for ~100 commands a day, but your voice then goes to that company. Recommendation: stay local; consider a cloud
fallback only for commands that don't parse (§2.3).

### Decision models over an API: JEV, or d1 hosted?
JEV is TypeSafe AI's cloud decision model ("System One" API, launched September 2026, early access, English-first,
no fine-tuning); d1 uses the same request format and is also hosted (OpenRouter, Haimaker), as are d1-ultra,
Perplexity's Decider and Cloudflare's Clef. They answer in ~0.1–0.3 s plus network; general LLMs with JSON output
take ~0.7–0.9 s. Since the rules now decide ~96% of commands locally in under 0.1 ms, a cloud model would only
speed up the remaining ~4% (from ~0.65 s), at the cost of sending what you say to a third party. Recommendation:
stay local; if unusual phrasings still miss, fine-tune d1 on our own sentences (§2.2).

### Should your Liquid API key be a fallback for d1?
Yes, as a fallback, not as the main path (§2.5). Liquid's hosted d1 answers in ~0.2–0.3 s against ~0.7 s for
d1-3B here, is a bigger model (Decision Index 58.9 vs 48.6) and costs almost nothing: input only, $0.02 per million
tokens, so a ~1,500-token decision is ~$0.00003 and the $2 starting credit lasts tens of thousands of commands.
But Liquid's terms let it train on what you send (what you said, what's playing, candidate titles), and it has
no published rate limits or region. As a fallback it only sees commands when the local d1 doesn't answer
(stopped, crashed, the laptop busy), which is rare. It's also what an always-on box too small for d1-3B would
use. Its confidence scale differs from d1-3B's, so it gets its own threshold.

### Learning from mistakes: does it need an LLM? Would an LLM catch what d1 misses?
The loop itself needs none: "that's wrong" marks the last command; the panel lists marked and not-understood
commands with what was heard and decided; you pick what you meant, and one click turns it into a fixed phrase or
a sentence of your own test set (kept on the laptop). Those sentences can later feed a d1 fine-tune.

An LLM helps in two places:
- **Suggesting the fix** for each logged mistake (which action, which phrase), where speed doesn't matter.
- **Live, as the last step** for what rules and d1 leave undecided: ~0.7–1.0 s more, for those commands only.

It would catch part of what d1 misses. d1 only chooses between the options described to it; titles and numbers
come from the rules. An LLM reads both at once, so it copes with odd phrasings, and with garbled titles when it
is given candidates (your library, Continue Watching, search hits). It is weakest where many leftovers are:
misheard numbers and names (HASE: 0.949 correct on clean text, 0.818 after speech recognition, the errors in the
values). So it complements the rules; everything it returns is checked (the title exists, the episode is in
range) before anything happens. Pick: OpenRouter with Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite (thinking off, ~0.7–1.0 s, ~$0.0001
a call) or gpt-oss-20b on Groq (~0.6–0.9 s), with zero-data-retention routing and a strict JSON schema.

### Skip intro and recap: is there a reliable source?
For skipping when you ask, yes; for skipping by itself, only when the data passes strict checks (§2.6).
TheIntroDB (free reads, ~217k episodes) keeps timestamps per file length, so the agent can use the ones made on a
release of the same length, with IntroDB as a second source and the file's named chapters as a last resort.
Anime is well covered and consistent; western series often have one submission or disagreeing ones.

### Spanish and other languages: which speech model?
Parakeet TDT v3 (§2.7): a drop-in for v2, 465 MB, 25 European languages, same speed, slightly worse English (6.34
vs 6.05 % WER). It can't be told which language to expect, and on short or mixed clips it sometimes switches
language mid-sentence. So it becomes a choice in Settings next to v2 (English only), not the default, until it's
tried with your voice. Canary 180M Flash (154 MB, English/Spanish/German/French) is the option with a fixed
language.

### 1Password backup
I can't open 1Password from the Claude app (`op` needs privileges the app's processes don't get). The backup
script read the key and password back from 1Password and compared them with the originals before printing
"Backed up and checked", so that line is the verification.

### 2.2 The decision model (d1-3B)

**What d1 actually sees** (llama.cpp builds it like Liquid's reference `prompt.py`):
```
<state>


QUESTION:
<instructions>

Options:
A <description of option 1>
B <description of option 2>
…
Reply with the option code only.
```
- Option keys are shown only when a description is empty; question ids are never sent.
- The answer is a softmax over the option letters only: all probability goes to your options.
- llama.cpp's confidence is (p_max − 1/N)/(1 − 1/N); with 22 options, our 0.30 threshold ≈ p_max 0.33.
- Question types: `choice`, `noul` (yes/no), `score`; no multi-label, extraction or few-shot field; several
  questions per request, each answered from the shared state.
- The state comes first, so the options are re-read on every call: latency grows with description length.
- Known weaknesses (documented for Jev, the same API): a lean to the first option, literal reading, numbers and
  counting; "none of the above" options often fail.

**Our experiments** (d1 alone on the 107 original sentences, Radeon 780M):

| Options | Right | Prompt tokens | p50 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before: 11 short options | 84 (82 with options reversed) | ~134 | ~0.2 s |
| 22 options, 2–3 examples each | 98 | 630 | 0.84 s |
| **22 options, 1 example each (live)** | **100** | 470 | **0.63–0.65 s** |
| 22 options, no examples | 95 | 278 | 0.41 s |
| 22 options, name + no examples | 95 | 339 | 0.48 s |
| 22 compact one-liners with an example | 89 | 302 | 0.45 s |
| same, shorter question | 78 | 296 | 0.45 s |

Richer, self-contained descriptions matter more than brevity; the question wording matters too.

**Rules v2** (live): one d1 intent per action; pattern rules for "play / put on / watch / start + title" and
"title + season/episode"; more clean-up ("okay", "and then", "thanks", "un pause"), phrases and patterns.

**Advice from the research, not yet applied**
- Put context in the state: `{"command": "...", "screen": "player", "playing": {"type": "series", "paused": false}}`
  and tell the question to use it only to break ties. Resolves "play" (resume vs. continue), "next", "stop".
- Ask a companion yes/no question in the same request ("Is `command` a request for the TV?") instead of relying
  on the `none` option.
- Accept when p1 ≥ a per-intent threshold **and** p1/p2 ≥ ~2; for low-margin answers, ask again with the options
  reversed and average.
- A/B test the Q8_0 model against Q4_K_M (Liquid's examples use Q8_0).
- **Fine-tuning**: Liquid's cookbook has TRL and Unsloth LoRA notebooks for d1-3B (Banking77: ~2,700 rows, one
  epoch, ~30 min on a free T4; 83% → 88–90%). Shuffle option order in training examples; merge the LoRA; avoid
  Unsloth's `FastDecisionModel`. Liquid suggests 500–5,000 examples. Converting the merged model back to a GGUF
  that serves `/v1/systemone` should work (the converter keys on `auto_map → D1Model`) *(inferred from source)*.

### 2.3 Speech-to-text, end of speech, the remote

**Bottom line:** the 700 ms silence wait is the main cost, not the speech model. No local streaming model beats
Parakeet TDT v2's accuracy; cloud isn't faster from Spain.

**Local models**

| Model | Runtime | English WER (Open ASR / AA) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parakeet TDT 0.6B v2 (current) | sherpa-onnx offline | 6.05 / 6.4 | ~0.05 real-time factor here |
| Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 | sherpa-onnx offline int8 | 6.34 / 4.5 | 25 languages, auto language detection (Spanish titles?) |
| parakeet-unified-en-0.6b | sherpa-onnx offline; streaming in NeMo only | 5.91 offline | |

### 2.8 How the decision steps do on hard commands (measured 2026-10-22, evening)

`server/scripts/bench_fallbacks.py`: the same sentences through each path, nothing sent to the TV. "Hard" means
what reaches the models in real use: indirect phrasings ("can you make it so I can actually hear them"), titles
described instead of named ("la de los atracadores con máscaras de Dalí"), Spanish slang, non-commands. Fully
right = the right action with the right details; delay = where that path's own model step ran.

**40 fresh hard commands that nothing was tuned on** (`eval/hard_holdout.jsonl`):

| Path | English (20) | Spanish (20) | Delay p50 / p95 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rules only | 10% | 20% | 0 |
| Rules → local d1-3B | 70% | 60% | 0.85 / 0.86 s |
| Rules → Liquid's hosted d1 | 85% | 60% | **0.16** / 0.45 s |
| Rules → d1 → LLM (the first order) | 90% | 85% | 0.85 / 1.6 s |
| **Rules → LLM → d1 if unreachable (now)** | **95%** | **95%** | 0.78 / 1.0 s |
| The LLM alone | 100% | 100% | 0.77 / 0.85 s |

The one miss in each language was a rule deciding first and wrongly ("play it in its original language" taken as
a title; "ponme el siguiente capítulo"); both rules were fixed afterwards. On the 80 hard commands used to tune
the rules and the LLM's prompt (`eval/hard.jsonl`; so these numbers flatter): rules → local d1 75% / 60%, hosted d1
80% / 68%, the first order 88% / 88%, now 100% / 100%. Before tuning, the deployed order got 75% / 62%: d1's
confident mistakes ("can you make it so I can actually hear them" → unmute) never reached the LLM, the LLM's
schema had no timers, library or Twitch, and greedy rules played sentences as titles.

On 96 ordinary commands (2 per category of `eval/commands.jsonl`) every order gets 96/96: the rules decide 95 of
them in under a millisecond. The LLM alone would get only 69% there (it paraphrases titles and misses exact
values): it's the step after the rules, not a replacement.

**What it costs and when:** Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite through OpenRouter, reasoning at its "minimal" floor: ~0.75-0.8 s
typical, ~1-1.15 s slow; about $0.0006 a call (~1.3k tokens in, a short JSON out), only for the few percent of
commands the rules don't decide. Liquid's hosted d1 is 5× faster than the local d1-3B (0.16 vs 0.85 s) and more
accurate, at a fraction of a cent; it's the step after the LLM when OpenRouter can't be reached and the local d1
doesn't answer. Zero-data-retention routing (`llm_private`) works but Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite's only such endpoint
(Vertex) took ~0.8-1.7 s.

**Speech (Parakeet v3, now in use):** same speed as v2 (~0.25 s for 5 s of audio). On the two Phase 0 recordings of
"play the bear season 3 episode 2": v2 heard "episode 3" once; v3 got the numbers right both times but wrote "the
Bare" once, which the library's sound-alike match now resolves to your The Bear. Two takes are far too few: a voice
test with the remote (English and Spanish) is the real measure.

With a decision model first, it decides most of what the rules miss (d1 71 of 93) and the LLM only sees what it isn't
sure of: its confident mistakes stand. That order saves ~0.15-0.2 s on those few commands and costs 10-18 points.

**Is an LLM better than a decision model here?** On what the rules miss, yes, by 10-20 points: a decision model only
picks one of the 35 described intents, and the title, numbers and language are then pulled out of the sentence by
code, which fails on unusual phrasings ("the series with the chemistry teacher", "crank it up a notch"); the LLM
returns the values itself, knows titles, and reads misheard command words. Decision models win on speed (d1 at 0.17 s
through Liquid's API), price, choosing from a short candidate list (the agent's title choice), and on ordinary
commands without rules (86-88% for the best, where our extraction code copies values exactly) — but the rules
already decide those. Best roles: the LLM after the rules; a decision model as the step when OpenRouter can't be
reached (Liquid's own API is independent of OpenRouter) and for title choices.
